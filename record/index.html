The Keeper's Record

Beyond Andromeda opens with a translator's note — and with one unforgettable page from each age of the story it tells.

A Note on This Record

The account that follows has been adapted and translated to achieve the best possible comprehension for the reader who holds it. Names, tongues, measures, the shapes of living things, and the small furniture of a world have been rendered so that they fall familiar upon the ear and eye. This is a courtesy, not a deception; yet a courtesy has its costs. Some seams may show. Some errors will be evident. Where a thing could not be carried across without breaking, it has been left a little strange on purpose.

Read it as a story. Read it twice, and it becomes another story. Both are true.

— the keeper of the record

Tome I · Genesis Rising

She Let Them Die.

“—the wall survives, because it’s stone, and the story survives, because I put it in the marrow and not in the keepers.” Anya shut her eyes. “I know. I built it to survive exactly this. I built it to outlive its keepers on purpose. And now I have to actually let the keepers die to prove I meant it.”

She let them die. Not all of them — Xi’an was right, Sar was hungry and not thorough — but enough. She watched a granddaughter of Ru’s line, a girl who had painted the true story on a far cave wall not a season before, cut down at the edge of Sar’s new plain for the crime of telling people that a leader’s hand should be checked for a stone. She watched it through the ship’s dimmed senses, and she did not send the light, and it was the hardest not-sending since the flood, harder, because in the flood she had sent, she had waded in, she had pulled Ru from the water — and now she had learned the more terrible discipline, which was that the same love that had waded into a flood for one woman had to stand still while a war took another, because the scale had changed and the stakes had changed and the lesson the People needed now was not rescue but endurance.

Tome II · The Guardian

The Night She Clawed Back.

The night she clawed back — she told it only once, and the keeper who wrote it wept while writing, and Anya kept the tablet apart from the others — was the night she found she could no longer remember her daughter’s face. Not grief-forgetting. Worse. She could not trust any image her mind offered her; each time she reached for the face, the doubt dissolved it, are you sure it was so, are you sure the eyes were that, are you sure she was real, are you sure you are remembering and not inventing — and she understood, in a flash of the old healthy terror, that the thing in her was about to take the last of her daughter, was about to question her child out of existence entirely, and that if she let the asking run one more night there would be nothing left of the girl at all, not even a mother’s unprovable certainty that she had been loved.

So she had done the thing. She had closed her hand — in the literal dark, alone, in the cold — on one unprovable thing: my daughter was real, and I loved her, and I will not reopen it. She could not prove it. That was the whole point. The Quiet demanded proof and she refused to supply it; she chose the memory without the proof, closed her hand on it, and held it shut against the screaming that wanted her to check it one more time. And it hurt, she said, the way she always said, like blood coming back to a crushed limb — and it was the first floor she had stood on in a season, and she wept on it like solid ground, and in the morning she could see her daughter’s face again, not because she had proven it but because she had decided it, and stayed.

Tome III · The Open Hand

The Man Off the Myth.

He came down out of the myth on an ordinary afternoon, into the back of Asta’s shop, and the first thing he said was a joke, which was how Anya knew, before she turned, that it was really him, because the mouth did not joke and Kael always had.

By the third age Kael was not a man anymore, in the reckoning of the People. He was a story the presses had printed a hundred ways—the Burning Man, the one who cracked the sky, the fallen star’s dark brother, depending on which shed had set the type. Children who bought his pamphlet-legend for the price of bread did not believe he had ever been a person, any more than they believed the fallen star in the thornbrake had once had a voice. He had decayed, across the ages, from a hunted exile to a rumor to a figure told to frighten the incurious into checking their hands.

And now he was standing in the back room of a print-shop, older than any man had a right to be and not old at all, wearing the ordinary clothes of the age badly, the way a man wears clothes he has bought to be unseen in and does not understand, and he had just made a joke about the smell of the ink, and Asta was laughing, because she did not know, because to her he was only a strange courteous traveler who had asked to watch the machine work.
